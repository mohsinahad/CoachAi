How to Run a Youth Soccer Practice for the First Time

For first-time parent coaches  ·  8 min read  ·  Works for U6 through U12

Nobody put their hand up, so now it's you. You have a practice in three days, a group of kids who just want to kick a ball, and no idea how professional coaches actually structure a session.

Here's the thing: you don't need to be a soccer expert to run a great youth practice. You need a structure, a few drills, and the confidence to keep things moving. This guide gives you all three.

The Four-Part Session Structure

Every youth soccer practice — from elite academies to recreational Saturday morning sessions — follows the same basic structure. Stick to this and you'll never be lost.

1. Warmup 10% of total time

Get every player moving with a ball immediately. No stretching in a circle, no standing and chatting. Tag games, dribbling challenges, or even just "dribble around and don't touch anyone" — anything that gets heart rates up and every child touching the ball.

Goal: Everyone warm, focused, and with a ball at their feet.

2. Skill Drills 40–45% of total time

Run 2–3 short drills focused on the skill you want to develop that day — passing, dribbling, shooting, or defending. Keep each drill to 6–8 minutes maximum. Explain in 30 seconds, demonstrate once, then let them play.

Goal: Repetition of one skill in a structured but fun context.

3. Small-Sided Game 30–35% of total time

This is what the kids came for. Set up 3v3 or 4v4 on a small pitch and let them play. The game should have a simple rule that encourages what you were working on in the drills — for passing: "you must complete 3 passes before you can score". Rotate teams every 4 minutes.

Goal: Apply the skill in a real game situation.

4. Cool-Down 5 minutes

Bring the energy down. Light juggling, passing in a circle, or just a quiet stretch while you recap what you worked on. Finish with something positive — a player of the day, a moment you noticed, something they did well.

Goal: End on a high note. They should leave wanting to come back.

Before You Arrive: The 15-Minute Prep

You don't need hours of preparation. The night before, answer three questions:

  1. What is the one skill I want to focus on? Pick one — passing, dribbling, shooting, or defending. Not all of them. One.
  2. What are my two drills? Look them up, write a one-sentence setup description, and one coaching point for each.
  3. What is my small-sided game rule? One rule that makes the focus skill rewarding to use.
Write your plan on your phone or a piece of paper and keep it in your pocket. Glancing at it during the session is not a sign of weakness — it means you prepared.

What to Bring

  • Enough balls for every player (or at least one between two)
  • 20–30 cones to mark grids and goals
  • Coloured bibs or pinnies for the small-sided game
  • Water bottles reminder (tell parents in advance)
  • Your plan written down

How to Explain a Drill

This is where most first-time coaches lose the kids. Long explanations kill energy. Use this formula:

  1. Show the space first. "We're playing in this square — from that cone to that cone."
  2. Say one sentence of rules. "You dribble through the cones and back as fast as you can."
  3. Demonstrate with one player. Show, don't just tell.
  4. Start immediately. "Go!" Any questions get answered while they're playing.

If you spend more than 45 seconds explaining, you've lost half the group. Get them moving first.

Handling Common Problems

Kids aren't listening

Use a signal — a whistle, clapping twice, or calling "freeze!" — and practice it at the start of the session. Tell them: "When I blow the whistle, stop, look at me, ball under your foot." Drill this once and use it consistently.

The drill isn't working

Move on. If a drill isn't engaging after 3 minutes, abandon it and go to the next one. Nobody knows your plan but you.

One player is dominating and others aren't getting touches

Add a rule: "You can only touch the ball twice before passing." This instantly redistributes involvement without calling out the dominant player.

Players are bored

Add competition. Turn any drill into a race or add a points system. Kids at every age respond to "first team to 5 wins."

Age-Specific Tips

U6–U8: Every child must have a ball or be moving at all times. No drills with lines and waiting. Switch activities every 5–6 minutes. One coaching point per drill maximum. The session should feel like play.

U10–U12: They're ready for tactics and decision-making. Use 2v1 and 3v2 situations. They respond well to competition — scoreboards, points, mini-tournaments. Coaching points should be specific and observable: "receive with your back foot so you can see the field."

U14+: Treat them closer to adults. They can handle longer explanations and more complex drills. Focus on shape, positioning, and reading the game.

A 60-Minute Session Template

  • 0–8 min: Warmup with a ball (dribble tag or similar)
  • 8–18 min: Skill drill 1
  • 18–28 min: Skill drill 2
  • 28–50 min: Small-sided game (rotate teams every 4 min)
  • 50–60 min: Cool-down + debrief
Your first practice will feel chaotic. That is completely normal. By your third session you'll have a rhythm and the kids will know your signals. Keep showing up — consistency matters more than perfection.
