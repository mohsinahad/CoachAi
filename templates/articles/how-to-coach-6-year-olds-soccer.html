How to Coach 6 Year Olds in Soccer: A Survival Guide

For first-time coaches of U6/U7  ·  7 min read  ·  No experience required

Coaching six-year-olds is less like coaching and more like herding a swarm of bees who all want the ball at the same time. If your first practice felt like chaos, you're doing it right — that's what this age looks like.

The good news: success at this age has almost nothing to do with soccer knowledge. It's about energy, patience, and keeping kids moving. Here's how to do it.

Your only real job at age six: make them love the game and want to come back next week. Everything else is a bonus.

What 6 Year Olds Can Actually Do

Understanding what's developmentally realistic saves you from frustration. At six, kids:

  • Can run, kick, chase, and dribble (sort of). They love having their own ball.
  • Can't grasp positions, "spreading out," or passing strategy. These are abstract ideas their brains aren't ready for.
  • Will swarm the ball in a giant cluster. This is completely normal and you should not try to fight it too hard.
  • Have an attention span of about 5 minutes per activity, often less.

Asking a six-year-old to "play left midfield" is like asking them to do your taxes. Keep everything concrete, physical, and immediate.

The Five Golden Rules

1. Every kid gets a ball

No sharing, no lines, no waiting for a turn. A six-year-old standing in a line is a six-year-old picking grass or wandering off. One ball per child means everyone is busy.

2. Explain in under 20 seconds

Their attention is gone after about three sentences. Show them the game, don't describe it. Demonstrate, say "go," and fix things as they play.

3. Use games, not drills

Anything with a name, a story, or a "monster" works. "Dribble away from the shark!" beats "practice your dribbling" every time. Imagination is your best coaching tool.

4. One coaching point, repeated

Pick one thing — usually "keep the ball close" — and say it cheerfully all session. Don't try to fix everything. They can absorb one idea at a time.

5. Praise constantly

Effort, not outcome. "Great running!" "Nice try!" "I love how hard you're working!" At six, confidence and fun are the entire curriculum.

A Simple 45-Minute Practice

Six-year-olds don't need more than 45 minutes. Here's a structure that works:

  1. Free dribbling (5 min): Everyone with a ball in a small grid, just getting comfortable. Add "dribble fast / dribble slow" calls.
  2. Dribble Tag (7 min): A couple of taggers, everyone else dribbling and dodging. Pure fun, lots of movement.
  3. Red Light, Green Light (6 min): Teaches stopping the ball without it feeling like a lesson.
  4. Sharks and Minnows (7 min): Dribble across the grid past the "sharks." They'll be screaming with joy.
  5. Small-sided game (15 min): 3v3 on tiny goals, no goalkeepers. Expect a swarm. Let them play.
  6. Cooldown (5 min): Each kid tries to keep the ball off the ground while you wrap up with high-fives.

Handling the Common Problems

They all swarm the ball

This is normal and mostly fine at six. If you want to ease it, use smaller teams (3v3) and more space, or play games where everyone has their own ball. Don't lecture about "spreading out" — they won't get it yet.

One kid is crying or won't join in

Don't force it. Let them sit with a parent and watch. Most kids join back in within a few minutes once they see the others having fun. Never make a big deal of it.

Total chaos, nobody's listening

Use a clear signal — "everybody freeze, ball under your foot!" — and practice it like a game at the start. When it falls apart, reset with the signal, then launch straight into the next fun activity. Movement fixes most behavior problems.

A parent wants you to be more "serious"

Politely explain that at this age, the goal is loving the game. Kids who have fun at six keep playing at twelve. That's the real win, and the research backs it up.
